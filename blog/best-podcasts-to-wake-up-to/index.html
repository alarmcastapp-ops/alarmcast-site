Podcasts that work as an alarm

The deciding factor is the time the episode lands. We measured publication times and episode lengths from 30 publishers' own feeds on 21 August 2026.

By the Alarmcast team, published 21 August 2026, updated 14 October 2026, about 6 minutes

Pick a daily news show as your alarm and there is a decent chance you will wake up to yesterday's episode, because you set the alarm for 6 am and today's went out at 6:30.

A show's publication time decides whether it can be your alarm, and it varies by hours between shows that look interchangeable.

The short answer

For a 6 am alarm you want a show that publishes in the small hours of your own timezone. Of 15 weekday shows we measured from their own feeds, eight published by 05:30 local time, comfortably before a normal alarm.

Five landed between 05:45 and 06:00, which suits a 7 am alarm and is marginal for a 6 am one. Two that sound like morning listening are published in the late afternoon, so a morning alarm always gets the previous day's episode. Most comedy podcasts run 40 to 90 minutes and arrive at lunchtime, which makes them the hardest fit.

Why the clock matters more than the show

An alarm that plays the newest episode has to find one that exists. Alarmcast picks and downloads the episode roughly 30 minutes before the alarm, so anything published inside that window arrives too late for this morning and waits for tomorrow. Any app that streams at alarm time has the same problem in a different shape: it can only play what the feed is carrying at 6 am.

There is a second wrinkle. The schedule a publisher states and the time its feed actually stamps the episode are not always the same, and an alarm reads the stamp. One weekday briefing we measured says in its own feed that it is available at 6:30 a.m. Across its last 55 weekday episodes, the feed's timestamps had a median of 06:00, with the earliest tenth at 05:46 and the latest tenth at 06:19. It arrives earlier than advertised, and the time moves by about half an hour.

So the useful question about any show is what time yesterday's episode was stamped.

Daily shows, and when they land

The two afternoon shows are the useful warning. Both feel like morning listening, and both are published after most people have finished work, so as an alarm they will reliably hand you the previous day.

Feeds hold a few other surprises. Some daily shows put a second, shorter edition on the same feed in the afternoon, so if the morning edition is not out yet, an alarm asking for the newest episode gets yesterday's afternoon update. Some swap in a different, longer programme at weekends. And in August, one was running summer repeats.

If you do not want news first thing

Not everyone wants the state of the world before their feet touch the floor. The gentler shows we measured fell into three kinds:

  • Very short daily shows, the shortest under two minutes. They end before you are properly upright, which suits people who want a sound rather than a sitting.
  • Daily single-story shows on history or science, about 15 minutes, with no news cycle.
  • Weekly or fortnightly story and interview shows, about 17 minutes. Ordinary people talking to each other is about as far from a siren as audio gets.

A weekly show that publishes in the afternoon still works. It is sitting finished on your phone the next morning, so set the alarm for the day after it comes out.

Comedy is the hardest fit

Comedy podcasts are long and mostly weekly. The four we measured had median lengths between 39 and 89 minutes. Several publish at lunchtime, and a weekly show has only one new episode a week.

The day-after trick helps here too. A weekly comedy show that publishes late on a Thursday night is ready for Friday morning, which is arguably the right morning for it.

The shows that can never be late

A finished podcast cannot arrive late, because nothing new arrives. Finished series and long-running archives often carry hundreds of episodes.

Set an alarm to carry on through a back catalogue rather than to chase the newest episode, and the publication-time problem disappears entirely.

One caveat on old shows. When we checked the 385 feeds in Alarmcast's shipping catalogue on 7 August 2026, three were broken outright and 50 had simply stopped publishing. A show that has not released anything for two years is fine as an archive and useless if you were expecting something new.

How to check any show yourself

Open the show in whatever podcast app you use and look at the timestamps on the last five or six episodes, not at the description. You are looking for three things: what time of day they land, whether that time is consistent, and whether the gap between episodes matches what the show claims. Ten seconds of that tells you more than any list of recommended shows.

Then set the alarm an hour after the show's usual time for a week. If it is a show you actually want, the hour is worth it.

Questions people actually ask

What makes a podcast good to wake up to?

Mostly the time it publishes. For an early alarm, pick a show that publishes in the small hours of your own timezone, so today's episode exists well before the alarm needs it. Then length: at least 10 minutes, so it is still talking when you get up. Of 15 weekday shows we measured on 21 August 2026, eight published by 05:30 local time.

Why does my alarm play yesterday's episode?

Because today's had not been published when the alarm needed it. An alarm has to download or fetch an episode that already exists, so a show stamped 06:30 cannot be the content of a 06:00 alarm. Check the timestamps on recent episodes and either move the alarm later or pick an earlier-publishing show.

Can I trust the schedule a podcast advertises?

Not to the minute. An alarm reads the timestamp in the feed, and that can differ from the stated schedule. One weekday briefing we measured advertises 6:30 a.m., yet across its last 55 weekday episodes its feed stamps had a median of 06:00, and most fell between 05:46 and 06:19. Check the timestamps on recent episodes instead.

How long should a wake-up podcast be?

Long enough to still be talking when you get up, which for most people means at least 10 minutes. Daily news briefings sit between 13 and 28 minutes, which fits. Comedy and interview shows commonly run 40 to 90 minutes, so you will only ever hear the opening.

Do podcast publication times change?

Yes. These were measured on 21 August 2026 from each publisher's own feed, and shows change schedules, run summer repeats and shift with daylight saving. Treat the figures as a snapshot and check the show yourself before you rely on it.
